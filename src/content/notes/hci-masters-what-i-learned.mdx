Every designer's path looks different. I started by teaching myself, learning through a lot of trial, error, and long nights. It worked, but I felt like something was missing. So when I got the chance to study Human-Computer Interaction in Estonia, I took it. I wanted more structure and depth.

By June 2024, I had finished the degree and returned to my role as an enterprise product designer. It wasn't a reset. It wasn't starting from zero. It was simply the next step. But the reality of what the degree gave me was very different from what I had expected.

Over the past two years, many junior designers have asked whether studying HCI is worth it. Here is the honest version of what I learned.

## Knowledge matters, but application matters more

An HCI degree gives you a strong foundation, but facts alone don't make you a better designer. The difficult part of design is not understanding the theory. It's practicing it. It's taking ideas and shaping them into usable, effective solutions.

In school, I had the freedom to explore ideas without worrying about business goals. In the real world, everything is tied to outcomes. Engagement. Sales. Retention. Creativity only works if it creates measurable value. That shift is what truly separates academic design from professional design.

## A degree won't automatically impress hiring managers

During my job search, I realized quickly that the degree did not carry the weight I imagined. Some hiring managers even saw it as a potential drawback.

A comment I heard more than once: "Designers with academic backgrounds rely too much on process. We need people who can adapt fast and solve problems with whatever is available."

The truth is simple. A degree might get you noticed, but your portfolio gets you hired. The way you explain your decisions. The clarity of your thought process. Your ability to collaborate and communicate. Those were the things that made a difference for me, and most of them were learned outside school.

## What actually matters: craft, principles, and tools

If you want to grow as a designer, focus on three pillars.

- **Craft** — your ability to create polished, thoughtful work.
- **Principles** — usability, accessibility, interaction patterns, and how humans behave.
- **Tools** — the things that help you turn ideas into real outputs.

The designers who stand out are the ones who balance all three. Too much theory without craft leads to shallow work. Too much focus on tools without principles leads to surface-level design. You need all three working together.

The best designers strike a balance across all three areas. It's not enough to excel in just one. A designer who knows all the tools but lacks an understanding of design principles will struggle to create impactful work. Similarly, someone who focuses only on theory without sharpening their craft might find it difficult to bring ideas to life. And without a grasp of tools, even the best ideas can remain unrealized.
