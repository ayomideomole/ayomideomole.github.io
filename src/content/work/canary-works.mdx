Security teams under active attack don't have an alert problem — they have a signal problem. Existing tools generate thousands of events a day; analysts spend half their time deciding what's worth investigating. Canary Works started from the inverse premise: what if the product's first job was to tell you what mattered *now*, why, and what to do — and only then offered the raw stream beneath?

We built the concept around AI as triage, not autonomy. The model sat between the firehose and the analyst, scoring events for relevance against the current threat surface, then surfacing the top handful with reasoning attached. Analysts kept full control: every claim was traceable to the underlying events, every recommendation came with a one-click investigative path. We tested with three security teams across two market segments to validate that the framing held under live conditions.

What shipped in the prototype was a real-time detection feed where each event arrived as a small structured card — severity, reasoning, suggested action, drill-down. The interaction wasn't conversational; analysts didn't want a chat. They wanted scannable signal with provenance. The visual language stayed industrial — typographic, dense, low ornamentation — because the audience reads dashboards for a living.

The prototype validated meaningful compression in mean-time-to-decide across the pilot teams. The project paused due to a funding shift, but the framing — AI as triage, not autonomy; signal with provenance over chat — carried directly into the ThreatDown work that followed. The bigger lesson: when AI joins a workflow already overloaded with information, the design problem isn't "make it smarter." It's "make it editable."
