SEB's internet banking platform was being audited to comply with new accessibility regulations, and the transfer flow was where the gaps were widest. Fifteen years of incremental product additions had accumulated into a surface that worked for power users and quietly excluded everyone else — older users, screen-reader users, and anyone whose mental model didn't match the legacy banking categories. The work asked a question banks rarely give enough time to: *when the person using this is not a power user, what breaks first?*

Research, usability testing, and customer journey mapping surfaced five recurring failures: payment types were hard to distinguish, form labels used jargon, repeat transfers were hard to find, confirmation screens didn't actually confirm what was happening, and there was no graceful path to undo a mistake. Each was small on its own. Together they pushed users toward the call center.

The redesign rebuilt the transfer experience around clearer information hierarchy, stronger affordances for recurring tasks, and inline explanations where the form alone couldn't carry the weight. Every screen was tested with users with low-vision and motor impairments. The work landed inside the existing design system — no major overhaul, no new paradigms — so it could ship inside SEB's release cadence rather than around it. WCAG AA compliance was a floor, not a target.

The shipped flow significantly reduced support load on the "I can't find how to transfer" tickets and improved task completion rates across all user segments — including the ones the original product had quietly underserved. The accessibility patterns were later picked up by other parts of the bank. The bigger lesson: accessibility isn't a separate workstream. When you design for the user with the highest cognitive load, every other user benefits.
