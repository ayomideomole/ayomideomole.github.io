Contract signing tools have become bloated. What started as "get a signature on a document" turned into dashboards, integrations, billing tiers, and onboarding flows that feel less like signing a contract and more like setting up a SaaS account. Zesto's wager was simple: signing a contract should feel like signing a contract — not configuring software. Everything past that wager was about removing things, not adding them.

The product team came in with a long feature list. We pushed back, hard, on most of it. The question we kept asking: does this serve the moment of signing, or does it serve the company that built the tool? Anything that didn't survive the first question got cut. What remained was the path between three moments: see what you're signing, sign it, share it. The design surface had to make those three things feel like a single sentence, not three separate apps.

What shipped looked unfamiliar for a contract tool. The document was the UI. No left-rail dashboards. No multi-step wizards. The signing experience opened directly into the contract, with a small persistent control strip at the bottom for the next action — *Sign here* / *Send back* / *Share* — and nothing else. Status flowed inline. Receipt confirmation was an email, not an internal screen. We removed everything we could remove until further removal would have hurt.

Pilot users described the experience as "feels like email, not software" — a phrase that became internal shorthand for the bar. Signing times improved meaningfully against existing tools. The bigger lesson, and the one I keep coming back to: removing things is harder than adding them. Every feature that wasn't there had to be defended just as hard as every feature that was.
