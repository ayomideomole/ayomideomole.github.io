The cybersecurity industry was years behind on AI. While 70% of marketing and design tools had adopted AI assistance, security platforms still leaned almost entirely on rule-based automation. Security analysts spent their days triaging alerts and cross-referencing data — repetitive, mentally heavy work — and the AI surfaces available to them felt bolted-on rather than embedded. The core design challenge: establish effective human–AI collaboration in a field with no real precedent for trustworthy AI interaction.

We revisited Jobs-to-Be-Done frameworks against existing workflows and identified the highest-friction moments — alert triage, threat investigation, remediation prioritization. Analyst interviews validated two assumptions: AI could meaningfully compress decision time, but analysts would never accept AI output they couldn't interrogate. Trust had to be built into every surface.

Two complementary approaches emerged. The contextual approach embedded AI-generated insights directly into the pages where decisions already happened — vulnerability lists, threat detail views, remediation queues. The conversational approach gave analysts a dedicated space to ask the AI questions, request summaries, or explore edge cases. We shipped both: contextual for the 90% of decisions analysts made fluently, conversational for the why questions.

The visual strategy reinforced trust without performing intelligence. AI-generated content is always visually distinct — a subtle background tint, a provenance icon, a confidence indicator — so analysts never confuse it with system truth. Color, typography, motion, and iconography combine to convey intelligence and approachability while staying inside brand cohesion.

The outcome was a unified AI surface across the product that analysts actually used. Internal usage data showed measurable compression in triage time and a meaningful increase in analyst confidence when AI was present. The bigger lesson: in trust-sensitive domains, the design challenge isn't whether AI can help — it's making its presence legible enough that humans can decide when to lean on it.
